reframe: |-
  The project is finished. The client likes your work and asks the question every freelancer wants to hear: “Can we just keep you on each month?” There is real work ahead. The site still needs stewardship. Campaigns will need changes. Production problems will happen. Nobody wants to start a new project every time something small comes up.

  A monthly arrangement sounds like the obvious answer. Sometimes it is.

  The trouble starts when the invoice becomes recurring before the service does. “Support” can mean a broken form today, a vendor problem tomorrow, an urgent campaign request on Friday, and a “quick” integration next month. “Ten hours” tells the client how much time is on the label, but it does not tell either of you what happens when work is urgent, when a request needs discovery, when the hours are not used, when the client wants them carried forward, or when the relationship starts consuming more attention than the time sheet shows.

  That uncertainty gets paid from your calendar. Small requests interrupt project work. Response expectations create obligations even during quiet months. Coordination, stewardship, triage, vendor problems, and context switching disappear behind the visible coding hours. The monthly fee stays fixed while the work around it becomes harder to predict.

  The common fix is to sell a block of hours plus “support” and hope the usage averages out. That changes the billing method. It does not define the recurring job, the client’s rights, your obligations, the project boundary, or the amount of business capacity you are actually promising.

  A useful retainer starts somewhere else: with evidence that a continuing job exists. Some client relationships genuinely need recurring ownership. Others contain future work that should remain projects, ad hoc work, or a paid assessment. A valid result of this system is no retainer at all.

  When recurring service is justified, the Freelancer Retainer System helps you design the obligation before you sell it. You define what the client is paying you to keep true, what systems and work are covered, how requests enter the service, what counts as an exception or separate project, what the promise really costs, how much capacity it consumes under stress, and what evidence will later justify renewing, resizing, restructuring, or ending it.

  The result is not “monthly hours with nicer wording.” It is a recurring service that has a real job, an operating boundary, an economic model, a place in your calendar, and a way to change without improvising every time the client asks for more.

proof:
  title: A useful retainer system should be able to tell you not to take more recurring revenue.
  body: |-
    The fictional Redwood Roofing case starts with an idea that sounds perfectly reasonable: ten hours a month plus support. The system does not polish that package. It breaks it apart and asks what actually continues after the project, what belongs in recurring service, what still behaves like project work, and what Alex Morgan’s business can safely promise.

    The finished recurring service becomes a support-led hybrid around Redwood’s current production marketing system: defined stewardship, covered business-hours response and triage, and up to six hours of approved campaign and analytics implementation per service period. Those six hours are not prepaid labor and Redwood does not own a reserved block of Alex’s capacity. New systems, major redesigns, migrations, and other project-trigger work stay outside the recurring fee.

    Later, Redwood asks to make the planned implementation lane permanently larger. The demand is legitimate and a larger monthly fee would be easy to justify on the surface. The capacity test says no. The expansion would leave only 1.75 modeled monthly hours of remainder and would push the stressed-week scenario to 36.5 hours against 36.0 usable hours. The system keeps the six-hour recurring lane, routes peak overflow to separately approved project blocks when real capacity exists, and renews the current service unchanged.

    That is what the method is designed to do: protect the useful recurring relationship without letting the desire for recurring revenue turn every new demand into a permanent promise.

faqs:
  - question: Should every successful project become a retainer?
    answer: No. The first part of the system exists to stop that assumption. You test whether a useful continuing job survives the project, whether recurring ownership creates value, whether the work can be governed, and whether recurring service is better than credible alternatives. Project-only, ad hoc, paid assessment, internal ownership, insufficient evidence, or decline can all be valid results.
  - question: Is this basically a way to sell prepaid development hours?
    answer: No. The book separates purchased labor, planned implementation, stewardship, support response, managed responsibility, and genuine reserved availability because those are different obligations. A quantity of planned work does not automatically become a client-owned labor balance, and capacity is not “reserved” unless the service actually grants the client that right and the economics account for it.
  - question: What do I actually build while I work through the product?
    answer: >-
      You complete ten connected records for one real client relationship: the Retainer Opportunity Diagnosis, Service Architecture, Boundary and Request System, Economics Record, Capacity and Independence Guardrails, buyer-facing Recommendation and Proposal, Onboarding and Baseline Record, Monthly Operating Record, Exception and Retainer Health Record, and the final Renewal Decision and Retainer Operating Specification. Each record carries evidence forward instead of asking you to invent the service again from memory.
  - question: Does the system tell me what price to charge?
    answer: It teaches you to model the full recurring obligation and establish an internal economic floor before you make a separate commercial pricing decision. It does not provide a universal market-rate formula. The Redwood dollar amounts are fictional teaching values used to make the reasoning visible, not a benchmark for your own client.
  - question: What if the client keeps asking for work outside the current service?
    answer: Do not quietly absorb it. The system gives you project triggers and commercial routes so a request can be included, reprioritized, separately approved, moved to a project or assessment, deferred, or declined. If the new demand is genuinely recurring, you reopen the affected economics and capacity decisions before turning it into a permanent client right.
  - question: What happens when the client wants a bigger retainer?
    answer: More demand is evidence, not automatic permission to expand. You test whether the larger obligation still fits the recurring job, economics, response design, calendar, future commitments, and stressed capacity. In the fictional Redwood case, a legitimate request to expand planned implementation from six to ten hours fails the current peak-capacity gate, so the base service stays unchanged and peak work uses separately approved project blocks when capacity exists.
  - question: Does this cover renewals and ending the relationship?
    answer: Yes. Renewal is treated as a fresh operating decision, not an automatic continuation or upsell. You compare the original design with what actually happened, decide whether to renew unchanged, resize, restructure, move work back to projects, or exit, and define the operating end state that follows from the actual agreement and applicable rules.
  - question: Is the Retainer Operating Specification a contract?
    answer: No. It is the internal operating authority that consolidates the current recurring job, service architecture, boundaries, response rules, economics, capacity decisions, baseline, operating rhythm, exception rules, and renewal state. The product is educational material, not legal, tax, accounting, security, worker-classification, insurance, or jurisdiction-specific contract advice. Use the actual agreement, applicable rules, current authoritative sources, and qualified review where those questions matter.
