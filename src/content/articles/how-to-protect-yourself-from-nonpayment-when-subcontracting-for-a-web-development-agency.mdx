In October 2026, a freelance WordPress developer posted an account of a project that had gone rather badly. They had completed a website for an agency, handled several rounds of revisions, received authorization to launch, and submitted an invoice for approximately AUD 600. Nearly two months after the launch, they were still trying to collect their money.

What makes the story interesting is that there was nothing particularly suspicious about the relationship at the beginning. The developer had been working with the agency since August 2024, and previous payments had arrived promptly. There was no indication of a dispute over the quality of the finished website, and the additional work requested during the project had been approved by the agency.

The website went live on September 2. The developer submitted an invoice the following day and was initially told that payment would be included in the next payment run. When that didn't happen, the explanation changed to outstanding matters involving the agency's customer. Eventually, communication stopped altogether.

The developer subsequently discovered that the agency's website was offline and that several people associated with the company appeared to have left. Whether the agency was closing, restructuring, or experiencing some other difficulty wasn't clear. The developer couldn't get a straight answer from anyone involved.

Unfortunately, there was another problem, and it was sitting in the subcontracting agreement. According to the developer, payment was due within 30 days after the agency received payment from its own customer. The developer had delivered everything required, but the event that determined when payment became due was happening inside a business relationship they had no access to. They couldn't verify whether the customer had paid, and the agency wasn't answering questions.

You can read the developer's account in the [original Reddit discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/buhaydigital/comments/1xfwo2a/freelance_web_dev_hasnt_been_paid_for_a_completed/).

I don't know whether the developer eventually recovered the money, and the account doesn't establish that the agency breached its contract. The circumstances do, however, illustrate a financial risk that independent developers ought to understand before taking subcontracting work. You can deliver exactly what you've promised and still discover that your ability to collect payment depends on someone else's commercial arrangements.

## The agency's customer isn't necessarily your customer

One of the attractions of agency subcontracting is that someone else has already done much of the work involved in acquiring the customer. The agency has an established relationship, understands what the customer wants, and is willing to pay you to handle some portion of the delivery. You don't have to conduct discovery calls with every prospective customer or spend your evenings trying to fill the pipeline.

For an independent developer who enjoys the technical work but has little interest in constantly selling, that can be a very agreeable arrangement. A handful of agencies with consistent demand can provide a substantial amount of work without requiring you to operate a full sales organization.

There is, however, a distinction between your relationship with the agency and the agency's relationship with its customer. The agency has contracted to deliver something to the customer. You have contracted to perform certain work for the agency. The obligations may be connected, but they are separate commercial relationships.

Suppose an agency agrees to build a website for $8,000 and hires you to perform $3,000 worth of development. The agency is responsible for its agreement with the customer, including whatever discovery, design, project management, and delivery obligations it has accepted. Your responsibility is limited to the work you agreed to perform for the agency.

The agency might invoice its customer in advance, collect payments throughout the project, or wait until delivery is complete. It might offer customers 30-day payment terms or occasionally extend credit to established accounts. Those are decisions the agency makes as part of running its business.

Now suppose your subcontract specifies that you won't be paid until the agency receives its own payment. You've become financially dependent on arrangements you didn't negotiate and cannot control. The agency has effectively passed some of its collection and cash-flow exposure down to you.

That may be perfectly acceptable if both parties understand the arrangement and you've deliberately agreed to it. Perhaps the agency offers enough consistent, profitable work to justify extending generous payment terms. Perhaps you've worked together for years, know its payment history, and have sufficient reserves to carry the outstanding invoices.

But I would want to make that decision consciously. I wouldn't want to discover that I'd agreed to finance an agency's customer relationship only after the invoice became overdue.

The distinction matters because developers sometimes evaluate subcontracting opportunities almost entirely in terms of the work being offered. They look at the technology, estimate the hours, negotiate a rate, and decide whether the project is worth undertaking. Payment terms get considerably less attention because they tend to look like administrative details.

They become much more interesting when you're the one waiting for the money.

## A 30-day payment term might mean a great deal more than 30 days

Consider two agreements. Under the first, the agency agrees to pay your invoice within 14 days of accepting an agreed milestone. Under the second, the agency agrees to pay within 14 days of receiving payment from its customer.

Both arrangements specify 14 days, which might appear reasonable if you're skimming through the contract. But they can produce very different results.

Under the first arrangement, you have some ability to establish when payment should arrive. You can identify the work required for the milestone, submit it for acceptance, and determine when the invoice becomes due. The agreement still needs a sensible acceptance procedure, of course. An agency that can postpone approval indefinitely could create much the same problem.

Under the second arrangement, your payment schedule depends on when the agency collects from its customer. Suppose you finish the development work on October 1, and the agency invoices its customer on October 5. The customer has 30-day terms but doesn't actually pay until November 20. Your 14-day payment period may not begin until that money arrives.

You could find yourself waiting until December for work completed at the beginning of October. If the agency's customer disputes the invoice or fails to pay altogether, the situation becomes considerably more complicated.

There are established legal distinctions between contractual provisions commonly called *pay-when-paid* and *pay-if-paid*. These have received considerable attention in construction contracting, where general contractors frequently engage subcontractors to perform portions of larger projects.

Broadly speaking, a pay-when-paid provision concerns the timing of the contractor's payment obligation. A pay-if-paid provision attempts to make the obligation itself conditional on the contractor receiving payment from the customer.

That distinction can have substantial consequences, although the legal treatment varies by jurisdiction and depends on the wording of the agreement. Some jurisdictions restrict conditional payment provisions, while others may enforce carefully drafted conditions. A clause that refers to payment following customer collection does not necessarily eliminate the contractor's obligation if the customer never pays.

The [American Bar Association's discussion of these provisions](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/construction_industry/resources/under-construction/archive/pay-if-paid-vs-pay-when-paid-clauses/) explains the distinction in the context of construction law. The principles are useful for understanding the financial issue, but the legal conclusions should not be applied automatically to software-development contracts. The governing law and actual agreement still need to be examined.

Fortunately, a developer doesn't need to become a contract lawyer to recognize the commercial exposure. The important thing is to establish what event makes payment due and whether that event is something you can reasonably verify.

If payment depends on the agency receiving money from its customer, I would want to know what happens when the customer pays late, disputes an invoice, or never pays at all. I would also want to know whether the agency has a definite obligation to pay me after some reasonable period, regardless of how its own collection efforts turn out.

An agency that cannot answer those questions is asking you to accept financial uncertainty without telling you how much.

## Negotiate the exposure before you accept the project

My preference would be to connect payment to the work I've agreed to deliver, with a clear deadline after each payment event. The agency's own collection arrangements would remain its responsibility unless we'd expressly negotiated something different.

That doesn't mean demanding full payment in advance for every subcontract. There are plenty of legitimate reasons for agencies to pay after reviewing work, particularly when dealing with unfamiliar contractors. An agency needs to verify that the agreed deliverables have been completed satisfactorily, and it may have its own administrative procedures for processing invoices.

The question is how much completed work you're prepared to leave unpaid while that process takes place.

Consider a $3,000 website implementation. If you agree to receive the entire amount after launch, you might spend several weeks developing the website, making revisions, and preparing the production deployment before receiving anything. By the time the invoice goes out, you've already committed the labor and surrendered most of your ability to limit further exposure.

Now imagine dividing the engagement into an initial payment of $1,000 before development begins, another $1,000 when the staging implementation satisfies the agreed requirements, and the final $1,000 after acceptance but before production launch.

Those figures are illustrative. A complex application with uncertain requirements might need a very different arrangement, and an established agency relationship may justify more generous terms.

The advantage is that you're collecting money as the work progresses. If each payment must clear before you proceed to the next substantial stage, you're limiting how much additional unpaid work can accumulate. The actual protection depends on the size and effort of the milestones, which is why blindly dividing every project into equal installments isn't particularly useful.

There's another consideration that tends to get overlooked. Payment milestones need clearly defined completion conditions.

Suppose the agency agrees to pay you when the staging website is approved. You finish the work, but its customer doesn't review the staging environment for three weeks. Eventually, someone asks for a different page layout and another round of content changes. The agency declines to approve your milestone because its customer isn't satisfied yet.

You've managed to create a payment dependency that's only slightly different from the one you were trying to avoid.

Acceptance should therefore be based on requirements that both parties can evaluate. If you've agreed to implement five pages according to approved designs, the agreement should establish how that work will be reviewed and who can authorize acceptance. If the customer subsequently wants additional pages or a different design, those requests need to be evaluated against the original scope rather than silently extending the payment milestone.

We've covered this broader issue in DBL's [article on defining acceptance criteria](/articles/how-to-define-acceptance-criteria-freelance-web-development-project/). The principle applies equally well to agency work, although the agency may have an additional review process involving its own customer.

Australian government guidance on [preparing contractor agreements](https://business.gov.au/people/contractors/prepare-a-contract) recommends specifying the payment amounts, due dates, milestone requirements, and conditions for acceptable work. Those are useful commercial disciplines regardless of whether you're dealing directly with the end customer or supplying services through an intermediary.

Of course, an agency may refuse the payment arrangement you propose. Some smaller agencies don't have enough working capital to pay subcontractors independently of customer collections. Others have established procedures that they aren't particularly interested in changing for one developer.

I wouldn't automatically treat that as evidence of dishonesty. I'd ask what flexibility exists and evaluate the project accordingly.

Perhaps the agency can fund the initial development milestone while using customer payments to cover later work. Perhaps it can agree to a definite outside payment deadline even if its normal practice is to pay sooner after receiving customer funds. You might also be able to structure a larger project into smaller engagements so that neither party has to commit a substantial amount of money or capacity before the relationship has demonstrated that it works.

There are circumstances where I might accept less favorable terms, particularly from an established partner with a dependable payment history. But I would want the economics of the engagement to justify the additional exposure.

A $600 invoice that remains outstanding for an extra month is inconvenient. A $15,000 invoice that remains unpaid after you've devoted six weeks to a project could have serious consequences for your business. The technical work may be identical in quality, but the financial implications are vastly different.

That's why I think subcontracting terms need to be evaluated alongside the project rate. The number on the invoice doesn't tell you everything about what the engagement is worth. You also need to consider how much time and money you'll commit before getting paid, how predictable the payment process is, and what happens if the agency encounters financial trouble.

An agency that consistently provides work at attractive rates can still be a poor commercial partner if it routinely leaves you carrying substantial unpaid balances.

## What if you've already delivered the work?

The developer in the original discussion had a more immediate problem. The website was live, the invoice had been submitted, and the agency had become difficult to contact. At that stage, negotiating a better payment schedule for the completed project wasn't an option.

The first task in that situation is to establish the contractual position as accurately as possible. I'd gather the agreement, invoices, records of the completed work, approval communications, and correspondence about payment. If there were revisions or additional hours authorized during delivery, I'd preserve those approvals as well.

Those records help establish what work was agreed upon, what was delivered, and what the agency acknowledged receiving. They also become important if the dispute eventually requires professional advice or formal collection proceedings.

Then I'd examine the payment provision carefully. Has the contractual payment event occurred? Is the invoice actually overdue under the agreement? Does the contract establish a dispute process or specify which jurisdiction's law governs the relationship?

In the Reddit case, the developer reportedly contacted the agency's customer to ask whether it had paid. I understand the impulse, especially when the agency has stopped answering messages. But a subcontractor should be careful about approaching the end customer directly. That business may have no contractual obligation to the developer, and the subcontract may contain confidentiality or non-circumvention provisions that affect what contact is permitted.

If payment is legally due, a written demand identifying the invoice, amount outstanding, relevant contractual obligation, and requested payment deadline may be an appropriate next step. Depending on the circumstances, further options could include dispute resolution, debt recovery procedures, or legal action.

The Australian government's [guidance on unpaid invoices](https://business.gov.au/finance/financial-trouble/what-to-do-when-you-havent-been-paid) describes a progression from reviewing the contract and sending reminders to formal demands and dispute-resolution options. The available remedies will depend on the parties' locations, the governing law, and the agreement itself.

With an invoice worth approximately AUD 600, the cost of pursuing recovery deserves consideration. A developer can have a legitimate claim and still face a situation where extensive collection efforts would cost more than the amount outstanding. That doesn't excuse nonpayment, but it does affect which remedies make financial sense.

I would also avoid attempting to force payment by disabling the finished website, deleting files, or interfering with the customer's systems. Apart from the obvious professional problems, those actions could expose the developer to claims from parties who weren't responsible for the original payment dispute.

Once an agreement has gone wrong, recovering the money becomes a separate commercial problem. The original development work is already finished, and creating additional liabilities is unlikely to improve the outcome.

## Agency partnerships need to make financial sense

I still consider subcontracting a worthwhile business model for independent developers. A good agency can provide consistent demand, handle much of the customer-facing work, and allow a specialist to earn a respectable living without constantly looking for new clients.

The relationships I've been discussing don't have to become adversarial. An agency has legitimate interests in managing project risk and protecting its own cash flow, just as the subcontractor does. Good agreements recognize those interests and establish responsibilities that both parties can reasonably fulfill.

But I would pay close attention to any arrangement that makes the developer responsible for financial events occurring outside the developer's control.

Before accepting a subcontract, I want to know what I'm being paid, what work earns that payment, and when the money becomes due. If the agency's customer is part of that calculation, I want to understand exactly how. I also want to know what happens if the customer doesn't pay, because that's when an apparently routine contract provision becomes important.

The developer whose experience prompted this article may ultimately receive the outstanding money. Perhaps the agency is experiencing a temporary disruption. Perhaps its customer has delayed payment. We don't have enough information to determine what happened.

What we can say is that the developer completed the work without having reliable visibility into the event controlling the payment deadline. That's a commercial position I would prefer to avoid.

The next time an agency offers you a project, spend as much care examining the financial arrangement as you spend estimating the development work. A profitable rate doesn't help much if collecting the money depends on an arrangement you don't understand and cannot influence.
