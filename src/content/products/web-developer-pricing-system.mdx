<ProductSection eyebrow="The moment every developer knows" title="The project sounds good. Then they ask: ‘What will this cost?’">

You know how to build the thing.

That is not the part making you hesitate.

The hesitation starts when you have to attach a number to it.

You think about how many hours the build might take. You think about what other developers charge. You think about the client's budget. You think about whether a higher number will kill the deal.

Then you choose something that feels **high enough to be worth doing, but low enough that the prospect might still say yes**.

Sometimes it works.

Sometimes the project that looked like a $6,000 win turns into six weeks of meetings, revisions, content chasing, deployment problems, undocumented integrations, and “one quick change” requests.

The invoice did not change.

Your effective pay did.

**That is not a confidence problem. It is what happens when there is no pricing system behind the quote.**

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="Stop pricing from fear" title="You should know the lowest number your business can rationally accept before the client ever tells you their budget." tone="muted">

If your pricing starts with “What do you think they will pay?”, the client becomes the center of your economics.

That is backwards.

Before deciding what the market may support, you need to know what **your business must support**.

A solo developer does not get forty clean billable hours every week. You sell, scope, answer messages, write proposals, maintain your tools, handle administration, learn, troubleshoot, invoice, and sometimes have gaps between projects.

Your project revenue also has to support more than your personal take-home pay.

Once you know your required annual revenue and realistic revenue-producing capacity, you have something most freelancers never calculate: a **Minimum Sustainable Rate**.

You may never show it to a client.

But now you know when a project budget is genuinely incompatible with your business instead of wondering whether you simply need to negotiate harder.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="The hidden reason good projects go bad" title="You are not selling coding hours. You are accepting responsibility for an entire engagement.">

A thirty-hour build is rarely a thirty-hour project.

There was the call before the proposal. The planning. The content cleanup. The messages. The stakeholder questions. The responsive states. The browser testing. The form testing. The analytics setup. The revisions. The deployment. The DNS problem on launch day. The handoff.

If only the visible implementation makes it into your estimate, the project is underpriced **before you write the first line of code**.

The pricing system forces you to account for the whole engagement.

Not because every minute needs to appear on an invoice.

Because every hour the project consumes comes out of the same finite capacity you use to earn a living.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="Know when not to quote" title="A fixed price is a promise about uncertainty. Do not make that promise before you know what you are accepting." tone="muted">

A prospect says:

> “We just need you to connect the site to our CRM.”

That could be a straightforward webhook.

Or it could mean undocumented authentication, inconsistent field mapping, duplicate-contact rules, missing credentials, legacy automation, rate limits, and a vendor API nobody on the client side understands.

Both requests fit into one sentence.

They are not the same commercial risk.

The system gives you a better response than padding every uncertain estimate with a random percentage.

When the work is understood and bounded, fixed pricing can give the buyer valuable cost certainty.

When the work is exploratory, time and materials may allocate risk more honestly.

When you cannot responsibly price implementation yet, **paid discovery can become the first engagement instead of free investigation hidden inside the quote**.

You stop treating every project like it deserves the same pricing model.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="The number between ‘too low’ and ‘made up’" title="Your economic floor is not automatically your selling price.">

Knowing the minimum sustainable number solves only half the problem.

You still have to decide what the engagement should actually cost.

That decision can account for things such as:

- how predictable the work is;
- how much estimate risk you are accepting;
- whether the deadline disrupts other commitments;
- how specialized your solution is;
- what alternatives the buyer has;
- how important the problem is to the business;
- how strong your proof is;
- and whether taking the project blocks better use of limited capacity.

That does **not** mean inventing a giant number because somebody on the internet told you to “charge based on value.”

It means separating three different questions:

**What must this project generate to make economic sense?**

**What additional risk am I accepting?**

**What commercial price is justified by the actual engagement and market context?**

Now the final number has a reason to exist.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="Stop letting small words hide large work" title="‘One quick change’ is not a pricing category." tone="muted">

Clients are not necessarily trying to create scope creep.

They simply see the request from the outside.

“Add another location.”

“Can we change the signup flow?”

“Could this also sync with our old system?”

“Can you squeeze this in by Friday?”

Your job is not to become irritated every time the project changes.

Your job is to have rules before it happens.

A change can be compared with the agreed scope. New dependencies can be identified. Schedule impact can be made visible. Additional work can become an add-on, a change order, or time-and-materials work instead of disappearing into the original fee.

The same principle applies to revisions, rush work, additional pages, integrations, and other variations.

**You do not need to say no to every change. You need to stop saying yes without changing the deal.**

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="When they ask for a discount" title="Do not negotiate against yourself just because the buyer wants a smaller number.">

A prospect says the quote is more than they expected.

Without a pricing system, the temptation is immediate:

> “I could probably knock 15% off.”

But if the scope, timeline, risk, and responsibilities stay exactly the same, what changed economically?

Nothing.

A better response is to change the deal before changing the price.

Reduce scope. Remove an optional deliverable. Extend the timeline. Limit revisions. Separate an integration. Exchange the concession for prepayment or predictable volume when that genuinely matters to your business.

Sometimes the answer is simply that the client's maximum budget and the project you scoped do not fit.

That is not a failed negotiation.

**A project below your economic floor does not become healthy because you were afraid to lose it.**

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="Your cash flow matters too" title="A profitable project can still hurt you if you finance the client's build yourself." tone="muted">

A $12,000 project paid entirely at the end is not economically identical to a $12,000 project with sensible milestone payments.

You still have bills while you are doing the work.

Payment structure determines how much completed labor and project risk you carry before cash arrives.

The system helps you set rules for deposits, milestones, handoff payments, long engagements, retainers, and minimum project size so every new opportunity does not reopen the same negotiation.

You are building a one-person business.

You do not need to accidentally become your client's lender along the way.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="Recurring work without unlimited obligation" title="A retainer should reserve something specific—not purchase unrestricted access to you.">

Recurring revenue sounds attractive until a fixed monthly fee becomes:

> “Can you just handle whatever comes up?”

For a solo developer, that can become unlimited obligation attached to capped revenue.

A healthy retainer defines what the buyer is actually purchasing.

Maybe it is reserved capacity. Maybe it is a defined recurring service. Maybe it is advisory access.

Whatever the model, response times, rollover, overages, emergencies, exclusions, cancellation, and unused capacity need boundaries.

Your monthly price should reflect the thing you are reserving or delivering—not simply be a discounted pile of hours.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="The quote is not the end" title="Every completed project should make the next pricing decision less speculative." tone="muted">

Suppose you quote $8,000 for a project you expect to consume sixty hours.

Then you track the engagement and discover it actually consumed ninety-four.

That difference is useful.

Maybe the build was underestimated. Maybe revisions were poorly bounded. Maybe meetings multiplied. Maybe an integration should have required discovery. Maybe content delays created hidden coordination work.

If you do not measure the difference, the next quote will probably repeat the same mistake.

The system turns completed projects into pricing evidence:

**Estimate → price → deliver → measure → compare → diagnose → adjust.**

You stop raising prices because somebody told you that you “should charge more.”

You change them because your own delivery data shows what needs to change.

</ProductSection>

<ProductSection eyebrow="The finish line" title="The next time someone asks what you charge, you should not have to invent the answer in front of them.">

The goal is not to memorize one hourly rate or discover a magic project-price formula.

It is to build rules you can reuse.

You know the economics your business must support. You know what belongs in a complete estimate. You know when uncertainty requires a different pricing model. You know how to establish a floor, think through the commercial price, structure payment, protect scope, handle changes, and review the result afterward.

Those decisions become your **Personal Pricing Policy**.

And each live opportunity can be run through a **Project Pricing Worksheet** instead of a fresh round of guesswork.

So when the prospect asks:

> “What will this cost?”

You are no longer searching for the number that feels least uncomfortable to say.

**You have a process for arriving at a price you can actually defend—and a project you can afford to win.**

</ProductSection>
