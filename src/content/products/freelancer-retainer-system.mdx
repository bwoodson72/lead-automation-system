  A completed project often reveals work that will continue. Systems need stewardship. Production problems need a response path. Campaigns and releases create recurring implementation. Clients may value continuity instead of finding a new developer for every small change.

  That does not automatically mean the client should go on retainer. The first question is whether a continuing job actually exists. The next questions are what kind of recurring value the client is buying, what obligation you are accepting, what must stay outside the service, and whether your business can carry that promise safely.

You build request and boundary rules before the first exception. You test economics and real calendar capacity before selling the promise. Then you translate the design for the buyer, activate it against the real client environment, operate it, diagnose abnormal periods, and decide from evidence whether the relationship should continue, change, or end.

faqs:
  - question: Should every successful project become a retainer?
    answer: No. The system begins by testing whether a real continuing job exists and whether the relationship can support recurring service. A project-only or ad hoc future relationship is a valid result.
  - question: Is a retainer just prepaid hours?
    answer: Not necessarily. The book separates purchased labor, planned implementation, genuine reserved availability, stewardship, support response, and managed responsibility because those are different commercial products. The distinction changes scope, rollover, utilization, pricing, and capacity decisions.
  - question: What exactly is in the workbook?
    answer: The 137-page workbook contains ten implementation records matched one-to-one with the ten guide chapters. Each record includes the purpose, inputs, procedure, completed Redwood Roofing example, reader working area, evidence checks, and a decision rule or completion gate. It is designed to be printed, annotated, or completed in an editable copy rather than used as an interactive PDF form.
  - question: Does the system tell me what price to charge?
    answer: It teaches you how to model the full recurring obligation and establish an internal economic floor before making a separate commercial price decision. The Redwood numbers are fictional teaching values, not market-rate claims or a recommendation for what your client should pay.
  - question: What happens when a client wants more than the current retainer includes?
    answer: Reopen the affected design decisions. Test whether the demand belongs inside the existing recurring job, whether the economics still work, and whether real calendar capacity exists. The larger request may justify a resized retainer, a separate project, a temporary project block, a different service structure, or no change at all.
  - question: Does this cover renewals and ending the relationship?
    answer: Yes. Renewal is treated as a fresh operating decision based on the original recurring job and actual evidence. The final chapter covers renewing unchanged, resizing, restructuring, moving work back to projects, defining an operational end state, and updating the Retainer Operating Specification.
  - question: Is the Retainer Operating Specification a contract?
    answer: No. It is an internal operating authority that consolidates the current commercial and operating rules. The book is educational material and does not create legal rights, remedies, employment status, tax treatment, security obligations, or jurisdiction-specific contract terms. Use the actual agreement, applicable rules, current authoritative sources, and qualified advice where those questions matter.
